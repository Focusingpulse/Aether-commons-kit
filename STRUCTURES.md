# Structures — the map, and the traps

A plain-language guide to the legal structures our community keeps asking about: **LLCs, trusts, private membership associations, and 508(c)(1)(a) churches.** What each one actually does, what it does not do, the cross-border layer, and the promoter patterns to recognise.

**Read this before you buy anything from anyone.**

---

## Three rules before the map

**1. This is not legal or tax advice.** Nothing here tells you what to form. Every claim is sourced so a professional can check my work rather than take my word. Get someone in your own jurisdiction who will put their name on the advice.

**2. Name the goal before you name the instrument.** Nearly every expensive mistake in this space is a structure chosen for a problem it cannot solve. The instrument is the last decision, not the first.

**3. The name of a thing is not its legal effect.** The IRS says this directly: *"Mere association of the term 'trust' with a financial arrangement does not make it a legitimate trust for federal income tax purposes."* And **"pure trust," "business trust," "unincorporated business organization (UBO)," and "Massachusetts Business Trust" are not terms in the Internal Revenue Code at all** — the IRS names them as promoter vocabulary used to disguise what something is.

## Part 1 — Goal → instrument

The most useful table in this document. Find your goal in the left column.

| What you actually want | The well-established instrument | Where a church / PMA / trust genuinely fits |
|---|---|---|
| **Privacy of my personal information** | LLC + registered agent + a discipline about what you file | A private association's membership records are the one thing that is genuinely private. Wyoming/New Mexico LLCs do hide member names. |
| **Protection from a judgment** | LLC (charging-order protection), insurance, homestead, retirement accounts | **Almost never via a trust.** Funds moved once a claim is foreseeable are voidable as a fraudulent transfer. **Timing is the real variable, not structure.** |
| **Avoid probate** | Revocable living trust — ordinary estate planning | Same instrument. Boringly. |
| **Lower my taxes** | Retirement plans, entity election, actual deductions | **Not a trust.** Grantor-trust rules mean income follows the grantor. "Eliminate taxable income" is a listed frivolous position. |
| **Govern a community** | — | **This is the real fit.** A private association with member terms, dues, and recorded admissions is genuinely the right shape. |
| **A church** | — | Apt where a sincere religious gathering exists. Automatic exemption is real; the obligations do not go away. |
| **Non-revocable identity** | — | **Nothing here does this.** No trust, church, or association delivers an identity that cannot be revoked. That is a cryptography problem, not a legal-structure problem. |

**If your goal is in the "almost never" column, the instrument is wrong.** Buy nothing until you can say which row you are in.

## Part 2 — The four instruments, honestly

### LLC
Ordinary, well-understood, and usually the answer for liability and privacy. Formation is cheap; ongoing obligations are annual reports and franchise fees. A **charging order** is its real asset-protection feature: a creditor of a member can receive distributions but generally cannot seize the assets or force a sale.

**What it does not do:** it does not reduce your income tax (a single-member LLC is a disregarded entity — the income is yours), and it does not hide you from a subpoena. It is a liability shield, not a cloak.

### Trust
A trust is a real and useful instrument for estate planning, probate avoidance, and holding property across generations. **It is not a tax-elimination device, and the IRS is explicit that it never was.**

The mechanics that decide everything:

- If you keep control, it is a **grantor trust** — income is taxed to *you*, and the trust is disregarded. All revocable trusts are grantor trusts. Many "irrevocable" ones are too, under §§671–677.
- **A trust cannot generally operate a trade or business.** If a grantor, beneficiary or fiduciary materially participates, the regulations require it be treated as a corporation, partnership, or sole proprietorship.
- A genuine non-grantor trust means **you actually give up control.** A trustee holds legal title and exercises *independent* control. The IRS states the plain version: *"Under non-grantor trust arrangements, you must give up significant control over income and assets."*

**The tell that a trust is not real:** you still direct it. The IRS's own description of abusive arrangements includes trustees who hand back *pre-signed cheques, cheques accompanied by a rubber stamp of the trustee's signature, or a debit card* so the original owner can still reach the assets. If you keep the wallet, you kept the trust.

### Church — 508(c)(1)(a)
**What the provision actually is:** an exception from the *notice* requirement. Ordinarily an organisation seeking 501(c)(3) status files Form 1023. §508(c)(1)(A) excepts **churches, their integrated auxiliaries, and conventions or associations of churches** from that filing. §6033(a)(2) also excepts churches from filing Form 990. §7605(c) requires senior approval plus two pre-examination letters before a church examination.

**So the much-quoted claim is TRUE, with one qualifier that gets dropped:** churches *that meet the 501(c)(3) requirements* are automatically exempt and do not have to apply. The exemption is automatic; **the requirements are not waived.**

**What "church" means, as precisely as the IRS states it.** There is no statutory definition. The IRS uses fourteen characteristics, drawn from its own examination guidance and from court decisions, and published in Publication 1828: distinct legal existence · a recognised creed and form of worship · a definite and distinct ecclesiastical government · a formal code of doctrine and discipline · a distinct religious history · membership not associated with any other church or denomination · an organisation of ordained ministers · ministers selected after completing prescribed studies · a literature of its own · established places of worship · regular congregations · regular religious services · Sunday schools for the religious instruction of the young · schools for the preparation of its ministers.

**Three things about that list that matter more than the list does:**

- **It is not a checklist, and there is no minimum number.** The IRS's own training material says plainly: *"The word 'test' is misleading, as there is no minimum number of criteria an organization must meet."* It also records that **the IRS has never officially committed itself to the fourteen**, and that **no court has explicitly adopted them as settled law**. Anyone quoting "the 14-point test" as a threshold is quoting a heuristic as though it were a rule.
- **The criteria are weighted, and one carries most of the weight: a real congregation.** Courts consistently treat an established congregation, served by an organised ministry, with regular services and religious education, as the central characteristic.
- **And the case that closes the obvious loophole is the obvious case.** In *American Guidance Foundation v. United States*, 490 F. Supp. 304 (D.D.C. 1980), a married couple acted as ministers with their family in their own home, distributed taped religious messages through an answering machine, and coached their son each week in what was claimed to be Sunday school. The court held it was **not a church** — *"a married couple praying together in the physical solitude of their home did not constitute a 'congregation'"* — and observed that they had made no real effort to extend membership beyond the founder's immediate family.

If it is a sincere gathering with actual people, this provision is genuinely useful and straightforward. If it is one person, the fourteen characteristics will not save it — and the case above is the reasoning a court will use.

**And here is the half that gets left out.** The exception removes a *filing*, not the *law*. The obligations all still apply:

- No **inurement** — no part of the net earnings may benefit a private individual
- No political campaign activity; no substantial lobbying
- Purposes exclusively religious/charitable; no illegal activity
- Employment tax, UBIT, and information-reporting obligations all still exist

**The IRS is unusually blunt about this provision**, in its own training materials: *"There is no question that certain taxpayers have taken unfair advantage of these (and other) statutory provisions protecting legitimate churches. The Service has informed Congress that these provisions are limiting the Service's effectiveness in preventing individual taxpayers from using the exemption provisions as tax-dodges for their own benefit."*

And the First Amendment is not a wall. In *General Conference of the Free Church of America v. Commissioner*, 71 T.C. 82 (1979), the Tax Court held the IRS **can** inquire into a self-proclaimed church's operations to determine whether it actually qualifies — and that doing so does not violate the church's rights. **You cannot refuse the IRS's questions about how the organisation is run.**

**The honest summary:** 508(c)(1)(a) is real, it is useful for an actual church, and it is not a mechanism for making your personal income disappear. A church that is you, for you, is the pattern the IRS has a dedicated examination supplement for.

### PMA — private membership association
**The finding you most need:** a PMA is **not a recognised legal entity type** in any state's business-organisation statutes. There is no "PMA" you can incorporate. It is a *contractual and associational arrangement* — a set of agreements among members — layered on top of some actual entity (an LLC, a nonprofit, a trust).

**Where the idea comes from:** the often-cited authority is the associational-privacy line — *NAACP v. Alabama* (1958), *Thomas v. Collins* (1945), *Gibson v. Florida* (1963), *Roberts v. Jaycees* (1984). Those cases protect **the membership relationship** from compelled disclosure: the state cannot force an association to hand over its member list where doing so would chill the members' rights.

**What that does and does not mean:**

- ✓ It is real protection for **who is a member** and for the private ordering of an association's internal affairs.
- ✗ It does **not** create an entity form.
- ✗ It does **not** shield the association's *business activity* from tax law, employment law, consumer-protection law, or product regulation.
- ✗ It does **not** let a member take money out for personal benefit — that is inurement by another name.

**Read plainly:** a PMA is a genuine and useful shape for **governing a real community** — members, terms, dues, recorded admissions. It is **not** an invisibility cloak for a commercial business, and invoking the case law as though it were a corporate form is the most common error in this space.

## Part 3 — The promoter patterns

These are not hypotheticals. Every item below is from an IRS publication.

### The layered trust "package"
The IRS describes two standard shapes — a **domestic package** and a **foreign package**. Both work the same way: multiple trusts, vertically layered, each distributing income to the next, with funds moving between them through *rental agreements, service fees, purchase-and-sale agreements, and distributions*. The goal is to reduce taxable income to a nominal amount using inflated or non-existent deductions.

**Promoters charged $5,000 to $70,000 for these packages**, marketed through seminars, flyers, and the internet, targeting small business owners and professionals.

### The "§643 compliant" trust
A specific marketed structure — usually described as a **"non-grantor, irrevocable, complex, discretionary, spendthrift trust"** — claims that §643 excludes extraordinary dividends and capital gains from trust income.

**IRS Chief Counsel addressed this directly in AM 2023-006: there is no such exclusion or deduction.** The structure does not provide the claimed benefit. The promoters included attorneys, accountants, enrolled agents, and unlicensed advisors.

### The five questions that catch almost all of it

1. **Who controls it?** If you still direct the money, it is a grantor trust and it does nothing. The pre-signed-cheque test.
2. **Does any term in the pitch not exist in the tax code?** "Pure trust," "business trust," "UBO," "unincorporated business organisation." Those are promoter words.
3. **Is the promise about *tax* or about *structure*?** Legitimate instruments change structure. They do not promise outcomes.
4. **What happens if the structure is disregarded?** If the answer is "nothing, it just doesn't work," you have risked fees. If the answer is "penalties and back tax," you have risked more.
5. **Does the promoter also sell the tax return preparation?** The IRS names "tax return preparer services made available" as part of these packages, and says it will pursue **promoters and preparers** as well as participants.

### What it costs if it goes wrong

- **Civil fraud penalty up to 75%** of the underpayment, on top of the tax
- **Criminal: fines up to $250,000 and/or up to five years per offence**
- Supposed charitable payments that actually benefit the owner or family are **not** deductible (*Fausner v. Commissioner*, 55 T.C. 620 (1971))
- Participants are explicitly **not shielded** — the IRS states both taxpayers and promoters may face penalties

### Where to check a promoter

- **IRS Tax Shelter Hotline:** 866-775-7474 · irs.tax.shelter.hotline@irs.gov
- **Publication 3995, "Is It Too Good To Be True — Recognizing Illegal Tax Avoidance Schemes"**
- IRS "Abusive Trust Tax Evasion Schemes" pages — the facts, the talking points, and the Q&A

**The single most useful habit:** when someone offers you a structure, **ask them what it does NOT do.** A professional will answer readily and at length. A promoter will change the subject.

### "The Trinity Method" — a bundle sold as a doctrine

If you are offered a **"Trinity Method"** combining a 508(c)(1)(a) church, a private membership association, and a common-law trust, here is what we found researching it.

**The name is not a legal term.** Searching it against the structures it claims to describe turns up no legal literature, no IRS guidance, no case law, and no law firm using it. What the search *does* return:

- A **2010 blog post by Rajiv Pant** describing a "Trinity Method of Technology Management" — Creator / Guardian / Recycler roles for a technology team's tasks, inspired by the Indian Trimurti and a Harvard Business Review article about 17th-century pirates. **Nothing to do with legal structures.**
- A wellness company in Belgrade, an Arizona LLC registered in 2016, and an unrelated software-development methodology.

So the term appears to be a name someone applied to a bundle, not a doctrine anyone developed. **The three instruments are real. The "Method" is the packaging.**

**And if you have been handed a long analysis of it, examine the analysis before the claims.** The one we were sent is organised by *corporate role* — CEO, CFO, CTO, CMO, COO, then "solo entrepreneur" — which is the shape of a job-description template, not a legal analysis. It carries numbered citation markers with no reference list. Its source list is roughly seventy per cent irrelevant, and the irrelevant portion clusters on the **word** "trinity": a hospital system called Trinity Health, an OKR software company's "trinity" framework, a marketing author's "trinity" mindset, the 2010 technology blog above, plus a set of generic job-posting templates for church CFOs and CTOs.

**That is a detectable pattern and worth learning, because you will be handed more documents like it.** A model searched the *name*, anchored on unrelated material that happened to share it, dressed the result in a corporate template, and produced something that reads as comprehensive while citing almost nothing relevant. **The tell is the citation salad** — real government sources mixed in with job-description PDFs and a hospital system.

**Here is the part that is right, and worth keeping.** The same analysis says, correctly:

- the three components "cannot be arbitrarily combined to avoid regulatory compliance"
- "courts have consistently rejected claims that 508(c)(1)(A) status provides immunity from IRS scrutiny or creates separate classification from 501(c)(3) organizations"
- "self-help approaches or guidance from non-attorneys can result in serious legal consequences"

Those are true, and they are the most useful sentences in it.

**And the accurate read is the one you probably already had.** It is not one method; it is **three separate instruments, each answering a different question, combinable in many ways.** The reason it gets sold as a single "Method" is that a package is easier to sell than three independent decisions. **Splitting a bundle into its parts is how you find out whether you want all three — and usually you do not.**

## Part 4 — The cross-border layer

Applies to every member who lives outside the US and is considering a US entity. **The advice to "set up a Wyoming corporation instead of operating from where you live" usually has two expensive holes in it.**

### Hole 1 — Form 5472, and a $25,000 penalty
A US single-member LLC owned wholly by a foreign person is a *disregarded entity* — but since 2017 (TD 9796) it is also a **"reporting corporation."** So it must:

- File **Form 5472 with a pro forma Form 1120**, by April 15, **mailed or faxed** (it cannot be e-filed)
- **$25,000 penalty** for failure to file, an incomplete filing, or failure to keep records
- After 90 days' notice, **another $25,000 per 30-day period, with no statutory cap**, per related party

**It applies with no US income, no US bank account, and no US operations.** The trigger is a *reportable transaction* with the foreign owner — and **contributions and distributions between the LLC and its owner count.** Running a US LLC from abroad generates exactly those transactions.

This is on page one of the IRS instructions. It is not in the sales pitch.

### Hole 2 — you cannot incorporate your way out of where you work
**Indonesia** (and a great many other countries) applies a **place-of-management** test. A company is an Indonesian tax resident if it is established in Indonesia **OR has its place of management in Indonesia** — alternatives, not cumulative. So a Wyoming company managed from Bali can be an Indonesian resident taxpayer.

**PER-23/PJ/2025, effective 9 December 2025**, adopts substance over form and locates effective management where strategic decisions are *actually taken*. The standard illustration: board minutes signed abroad do not settle the question if the decisions recorded in them were made at home.

If a permanent establishment exists: 22% corporate tax **plus a 20% branch profits tax** on after-tax profit, whether or not repatriated. PE triggers include a place of management, an office for promotion and selling, services furnished by employees beyond 60 days in twelve months, a dependent agent, and computers or automated equipment used to conduct business over the internet.

**A US layer does not remove a local management presence. It adds a second jurisdiction's filing obligations on top of the one that was already there.**

**Also separate, and not fixable by paperwork:** immigration. Doing business in a country on the wrong visa is its own problem, and enforcement is real.

### And "one of us could take hold of it"
**Legitimate:** a **registered agent** (a state requirement, usually a service company), or an **appointed manager or director with real authority, a written agreement, and disclosure as beneficial owner**. Ordinary practice everywhere.

**Not legitimate: a nominee.** Someone holds the entity for another person, undisclosed. Banks, Stripe, and payment processors run beneficial-ownership checks, so the omission is where civil exposure becomes fraud. The nominee personally carries the liability, including the $25,000 penalties above. It can be tax evasion for both parties. And it weakens a private-association posture rather than strengthening it — an entity purporting to be membership-held while being controlled by one undisclosed person is a worse position, not a better one.

**The test:** does the holder have real authority, and is the arrangement disclosed? If the answer to either is no, stop.

## Part 5 — If you are going to do it anyway, do it like this

1. **Write down the goal first.** One sentence. If the instrument does not map to it in Part 1, do not proceed.
2. **Get a professional who names both jurisdictions** if you are cross-border. The trap is hiring someone who answers only the US half.
3. **Understand that substance beats paperwork.** Every one of these instruments is judged on what actually happens, not on what the documents say. A trust with no independent trustee is not a trust. A church with one member is a tax-dodge pattern. An association with no meetings, no minutes, and no dues is a name.
4. **Never move assets once a claim is foreseeable.** That is a fraudulent transfer and it fails regardless of how good the structure is.
5. **Keep the vocabulary out of anything public.** Sovereign-citizen phrasing on a website is a signal to auditors and it damages any real case you might have.
6. **Ask what it does not do.** Before you pay.

## Part 6 — What we concluded for ourselves

Offered only as a worked example, not as a recommendation.

We looked at this material in September 2026 — a platform selling a 508(c)(1)(a) church, a PMA, and a common-law trust as a bundle. Our conclusions:

- **The 508(c)(1)(a) no-Form-1023 point is true.** Verified against §508 and IRS training materials.
- **"PMA" is not a corporate form anywhere**, and its origin is a misreading of the associational-privacy cases. Real protection for membership records; not an entity.
- **A self-settled or foreseeable-creditor transfer is void** — not merely ineffective — under state fraudulent-transfer statutes.
- **"Eliminate taxable income" is a listed frivolous position** (Notice 2010-33 ¶24). It appeared in the promoter's *sibling* company, not on the platform's own site, which is worth noticing: the careful material and the overclaim came from the same person.
- **The one genuine fit** was community governance — a private association with real members, real terms, and real dues.
- **Nothing in the bundle delivered what we most wanted,** which was an identity that cannot be revoked. That turned out to be a cryptographic problem (`did:key`), not a legal one.

**The lesson we kept:** judge the engineering on whether it works, and quarantine the grand theory. Good software wrapped around a bad tax premise is still a bad tax premise — and the software being honest is not evidence that the premise is.
