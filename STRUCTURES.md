# Structures — the instruments, the documents, and the traps

**A briefing for the Aetherforce community.** Two questions came up after the talk: the **instrument** question (how do PMAs, churches, and trusts actually work) and the **document** question (how do we keep things for posterity without getting anyone in trouble). This is the written version of both, with the sources.

**Read this before you buy anything from anyone.**

---

## Part 0 — First, which law are we talking about

Three layers are stacked in this document, and they travel very differently. Getting this straight is most of the value.

**Federal — identical in all fifty US states.** The church rules (§508(c)(1)(a), §501(c)(3), §4958, §6033), the grantor-trust tax rules (§§671–679), the frivolous-position list (Notice 2010-33), the promoter penalties (§6700, §6701, §7408), and the general rule that *control plus benefit means you are taxed on it* are the same everywhere in the United States. **If you are in the US, this layer applies to you exactly as written below.**

**State — this is where it differs, and it is the layer that decides asset protection.** Whether a transfer is void against a creditor, what a homestead exemption is worth, whether retirement accounts are protected, whether your state recognises tenancy by the entirety, what an association may be, and what a trust must do to be valid are all **state** law. We checked Colorado in detail because that is where our work sits, and we use it below as the worked example. **Read the Colorado lines as a pattern, not as your answer.** The statutes change from state to state. The questions do not.

**Outside the US — a different system, not a variation.** The 508(c)(1)(a) church and the "PMA" theory are US artifacts. The trust, as a device that separates legal title from beneficial enjoyment, is a **common-law** institution; many civil-law countries do not have it in that form and use foundations or other instruments instead. **If you are outside the United States, do not read this as applying to you.** Treat it as background on how the US conversation works, and get advice where you live.

**The portable part is one sentence,** and it sits at the top of Part 1: *there is no lawful structure that lets you keep full control and full use of assets while making them unreachable by your creditors.* That is true in Colorado, true in the other states, and true wherever a trust exists. Everything else here is a worked example of what that sentence costs when you try to go around it.

## Part 1 — The instruments, honestly

**The one rule that decides everything.** There is no lawful structure that lets you keep full control and full use of assets while making them unreachable by your creditors. Almost every "go private" pitch promises exactly that. Every jurisdiction we examined says no. Everything below follows from that sentence.

### The 508(c)(1)(a) church

**True:** a qualifying church needs no Form 1023. [§508(c)(1)(A)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/508) waives the *notice* requirement and nothing else, and a church that genuinely qualifies is excepted from the annual Form 990 by [§6033(a)(3)(A)(i)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/6033).

**Not true:** that this shelters income. Exemption requires actually being organized and operated exclusively for religious purposes. **The exemption is automatic; the requirements are not waived.**

- **Donations are not automatically deductible.** [§170](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/170) turns on whether the organization *is* described in §501(c)(3). Without a determination letter, the donor carries the burden of establishing it.
- **Inurement is absolute.** You cannot draw church income or assets for personal living expenses. See [§4958](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/4958) and [IRS Publication 1828](https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-pdf/p1828.pdf).
- **Employment tax, UBIT, and Form 990-T still apply.**
- **It is not a records shield.** §7611 protects "church records," but bank records are third-party records governed by §7609 — see [*God's Storehouse Topeka Church v. United States*, No. 23-3063 (10th Cir. 2024)](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/ca10/23-3063/23-3063-2024-04-09.html).
- **The immunity pitch is the dangerous one.** A federal court called the 508-immunity argument "frivolous" in *Steeves v. IRS* (S.D. Cal. 2020), and the Department of Justice has permanently enjoined promoters selling it — see the [*Stoll* permanent injunction](https://www.justice.gov/archive/tax/Stoll_Perm_Inj.pdf), and the *Sinclair*, *Gardner*, and *Schrock* actions.

**What "church" means, as precisely as the IRS states it.** There is no statutory definition. The IRS uses fourteen characteristics, drawn from its own examination guidance and from court decisions, and published in [Publication 1828](https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-pdf/p1828.pdf): distinct legal existence · a recognised creed and form of worship · a definite and distinct ecclesiastical government · a formal code of doctrine and discipline · a distinct religious history · membership not associated with any other church or denomination · an organisation of ordained ministers · ministers selected after completing prescribed studies · a literature of its own · established places of worship · regular congregations · regular religious services · Sunday schools for the religious instruction of the young · schools for the preparation of its ministers.

**Three things about that list that matter more than the list does:**

- **It is not a checklist, and there is no minimum number.** The IRS's own training material says plainly: *"The word 'test' is misleading, as there is no minimum number of criteria an organization must meet."* It also records that **the IRS has never officially committed itself to the fourteen**, and that **no court has explicitly adopted them as settled law**. Anyone quoting "the 14-point test" as a threshold is quoting a heuristic as though it were a rule.
- **The criteria are weighted, and one carries most of the weight: a real congregation.** Courts consistently treat an established congregation, served by an organised ministry, with regular services and religious education, as the central characteristic.
- **And the case that closes the obvious loophole is the obvious case.** In *American Guidance Foundation v. United States*, 490 F. Supp. 304 (D.D.C. 1980), a married couple acted as ministers with their family in their own home, distributed taped religious messages through an answering machine, and coached their son each week in what was claimed to be Sunday school. The court held it was **not a church** — *"a married couple praying together in the physical solitude of their home did not constitute a 'congregation'"* — and observed that they had made no real effort to extend membership beyond the founder's immediate family.

If it is a sincere gathering with actual people, this provision is genuinely useful and straightforward. If it is one person, the fourteen characteristics will not save it — and the case above is the reasoning a court will use.

### The "private membership association"

**It is not a recognized entity type** under federal tax law or under the law of any state we examined. There is no PMA statute, no PMA exemption category, and no registry anywhere.

**Where the idea comes from:** a misreading of [*NAACP v. Alabama* (1958)](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/357/449/). **The Court's own words:** the NAACP "asserts no right to absolute immunity from state investigation, and no right to disregard Alabama's laws."

**Where it has been rejected as a shield:** *Lytle v. U.S. Dep't of Health & Human Services* (8th Cir. 2015), *United States v. Allgyer*, *United States v. Cole*, *Miller's Organic Farm*, *Mason v. State* (Ark. App. 2014), *Diruzzo v. State* (Tex. App. 2018). It confers **no limited liability** and does not displace licensing, health, food-safety, employment, consumer-protection, or liability law.

**The downside nobody mentions:** a signed membership agreement that recites the "private domain" theory is a paper trail a plaintiff or a regulator can use as evidence of intent. It can turn a routine business dispute into a fraud case.

**Where it is genuinely real:** governing an actual community — member terms, recorded admissions, dues, minutes, elections. That work is real and it has real value. The legitimate routes for it are a genuine [§501(c)(7)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/501) social club or a **state nonprofit-association statute**. Colorado's is [C.R.S. 7-30-101](https://colorado.public.law/statutes/crs_7-30-101); most states have some form of it under a different number. **That statute is the legitimate version of the thing the PMA theory imitates**, and it is worth knowing what yours is called.

### A trust

The four lines below are Colorado's, and they are the worked example. **The mechanism is the same in every US state** — every state has a voidable-transactions act and a fraudulent-transfer doctrine, and every state taxes a grantor who keeps control. The numbers and the labels differ. The questions do not.

- **Against a creditor you already have:** a self-settled transfer is "void as against the creditors existing of such person" — [C.R.S. 38-10-111](https://colorado.public.law/statutes/crs_38-10-111).
- **Against a future or foreseeable creditor:** [C.R.S. 38-8-105(1)](https://colorado.public.law/statutes/crs_38-8-105) reaches claims arising "before or after the transfer," and the badges of fraud include insolvency shortly after the transfer and a transfer made shortly before or after a substantial debt is incurred.
- **Taxes follow control.** [§§671–679](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/671) mean the grantor is taxed on the grantor's own trust. "Eliminate taxable income" is a **listed frivolous position** — [Notice 2010-33, ¶24](https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-drop/n-10-33.pdf) — and promoting it carries §6700 and §6701 penalties and §7408 injunctions.
- **Sham and alter-ego doctrines defeat it** when the settlor keeps dominion or ignores the formalities: *In re Cohen*, *Peters v. Bryan*, *Sparkman*, *Kaladic*.
- **Two real points, worth keeping.** Colorado has **no trust registration requirement**, and a certification of trust ([C.R.S. 15-5-1013](https://colorado.public.law/statutes/crs_15-5-1013)) can be given to a counterparty instead of the instrument. That is a genuine but modest privacy benefit. And **a trust must actually be funded** — deeds re-recorded, accounts and vehicles retitled. Anything not retitled is not in the trust; the document then describes property it does not own.

### The promoter patterns, from the IRS's own pages

Not hypotheticals. Every item below is from an IRS publication.

**The layered "package."** The IRS describes two standard shapes — **domestic** and **foreign**. Both work the same way: multiple trusts, vertically layered, each distributing income to the next, funds moving between them through *rental agreements, service fees, purchase-and-sale agreements, and distributions*. The goal is to reduce taxable income to a nominal amount using inflated or non-existent deductions. **Promoters charged $5,000 to $70,000** for these packages, marketed through seminars, flyers and the internet, targeting small business owners and professionals.

**The "§643 compliant" trust.** A specific marketed structure — usually described as a **"non-grantor, irrevocable, complex, discretionary, spendthrift trust"** — claims §643 excludes extraordinary dividends and capital gains from trust income. **IRS Chief Counsel addressed this in AM 2023-006: there is no such exclusion or deduction.** The promoters included attorneys, accountants, enrolled agents, and unlicensed advisors.

**The words that are not in the tax code.** "Pure trust," "business trust," "unincorporated business organization (UBO)," "Massachusetts Business Trust." The IRS names these as promoter vocabulary used to disguise what something is. The IRS says it directly: *"Mere association of the term 'trust' with a financial arrangement does not make it a legitimate trust for federal income tax purposes."*

**The tell that a trust is not real:** you still direct it. The IRS's description of abusive arrangements includes trustees who hand back *pre-signed cheques, cheques accompanied by a rubber stamp of the trustee's signature, or a debit card* so the original owner can still reach the assets. **If you keep the wallet, you kept the trust.**

**What it costs if it goes wrong:** civil fraud penalty **up to 75%** of the underpayment on top of the tax; criminal fines **up to $250,000 and/or up to five years** per offence; participants explicitly **not shielded**, and the IRS says it will pursue **promoters and preparers** as well. Supposed charitable payments that actually benefit the owner or family are not deductible (*Fausner v. Commissioner*, 55 T.C. 620 (1971)).

**Where to check a promoter:** IRS Tax Shelter Hotline **866-775-7474** · irs.tax.shelter.hotline@irs.gov · [Publication 3995, "Is It Too Good To Be True"](https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-pdf/p3995.pdf).

**Five questions that catch most of it:**

1. **Who controls it?** If you still direct the money, it is a grantor trust and it does nothing.
2. **Does any term in the pitch not exist in the tax code?** Those are promoter words.
3. **Is the promise about *tax* or about *structure*?** Legitimate instruments change structure. They do not promise outcomes.
4. **What happens if the structure is disregarded?** "Nothing, it just doesn't work" is fees. "Back tax and penalties" is more.
5. **Does the promoter also sell the tax return preparation?** The IRS names that as part of these packages.

**The single most useful habit:** when someone offers you a structure, **ask them what it does NOT do.** A professional will answer readily and at length. A promoter will change the subject.

### "The Trinity Method" — a bundle sold as a doctrine

If you are offered a **"Trinity Method"** combining a 508(c)(1)(a) church, a private membership association, and a common-law trust, here is what we found researching it.

**The name is not a legal term.** Searching it against the structures it claims to describe turns up no legal literature, no IRS guidance, no case law, and no law firm using it. What the search *does* return:

- A **2010 blog post by Rajiv Pant** describing a "Trinity Method of Technology Management" — Creator / Guardian / Recycler roles for a technology team's tasks, inspired by the Indian Trimurti and a Harvard Business Review article about 17th-century pirates. **Nothing to do with legal structures.**
- A wellness company in Belgrade, an Arizona LLC registered in 2016, and an unrelated software-development methodology.

So the term appears to be a name someone applied to a bundle, not a doctrine anyone developed. **The three instruments are real. The "Method" is the packaging.**

**And if you have been handed a long analysis of it, examine the analysis before the claims.** One circulated version is organised by *corporate role* — CEO, CFO, CTO, CMO, COO, then "solo entrepreneur" — which is the shape of a job-description template, not a legal analysis. It carries numbered citation markers with no reference list. Its source list is roughly seventy per cent irrelevant, and the irrelevant portion clusters on the **word** "trinity": a hospital system called Trinity Health, an OKR software company's "trinity" framework, a marketing author's "trinity" mindset, the 2010 technology blog above, plus generic job-posting templates for church CFOs and CTOs.

**That is a detectable pattern and worth learning, because more documents like it are coming.** A model searched the *name*, anchored on unrelated material that happened to share it, dressed the result in a corporate template, and produced something that reads as comprehensive while citing almost nothing relevant. **The tell is the citation salad** — real government sources mixed in with job-description PDFs and a hospital system.

**Here is the part that is right, and worth keeping.** The same analysis says, correctly:

- the three components "cannot be arbitrarily combined to avoid regulatory compliance"
- "courts have consistently rejected claims that 508(c)(1)(A) status provides immunity from IRS scrutiny or creates separate classification from 501(c)(3) organizations"
- "self-help approaches or guidance from non-attorneys can result in serious legal consequences"

**And the accurate read is the one you probably already had: it is not one method. It is three separate instruments, each answering a different question, combinable in many ways.** The reason it gets sold as a single "Method" is that a package is easier to sell than three independent decisions. **Splitting a bundle into its parts is how you find out whether you want all three — and usually you do not.**

### If you are looking at a platform that teaches this

There is at least one platform selling a monthly class plus software for exactly these three instruments. Two things said fairly: **its software is thoughtfully built** — its trust dashboard shows "property received: $0.00" and warns that an unfunded trust describes property it does not own, and its free structure-finder will tell you that none of the structures fits. Its own material states the inurement limits correctly, and its own page tells you to have a qualified professional review anything before you act on it.

Where the register changes is a **sibling company run by the same founder**, which markets *"eliminating taxable income"* and *"the highest level of asset protection possible."* That is the listed frivolous position, and it is the part to keep away from.

**Learn the pattern rather than the name.** A careful, honestly-built product with a reckless marketing arm beside it is more dangerous than an obvious scam — because the quality of the platform is what persuades you the tax claim must be sound too. It is not. The same person can be scrupulous about the software and reckless about the promise, and the careful half is the half that earns your trust.

### The part that travels: eight questions to bring to a local professional

Whatever your jurisdiction, these are the questions that decide the answer, and any competent local professional can answer them:

1. **What does my state (or country) actually have?** Is there a statutory form for what I want — an association, a nonprofit, a trust, a foundation — or am I relying on a model that has no statute behind it?
2. **Who is liable if someone is hurt or unpaid?** Does the structure give real limited liability, or just a different name on the same exposure?
3. **If I am sued tomorrow, what happens to this asset?** Is the transfer void against creditors I already have, and does the law reach creditors whose claims arise later but were foreseeable?
4. **Which rights am I not exercising?** If I keep control and keep using the property, what does the law say about that?
5. **What has to be filed, and what has to be retitled?** Registration, periodic reports, deeds, accounts, vehicles. **Anything not retitled is not moved.**
6. **What are the annual obligations?** Tax filings, employment tax, minutes, membership records, charitable registration.
7. **What is the failure mode?** If this is challenged and I lose, what does losing cost — penalties, back tax, personal liability, or nothing?
8. **What would make this a listed or frivolous position where I live?** In the US that list is public (Notice 2010-33 and its successors). Most countries have an equivalent list of structures their revenue authority has already rejected.

**If a professional cannot answer 1 through 8 plainly, that is itself the answer** about whether to proceed.

**This is education, not legal advice.** Check your own state, and get counsel in your jurisdiction before executing anything.

## Part 2 — The document rules, for posterity's sake

Our whole system runs on one distinction: **there is a private territory and a public map, and we publish the map.**

```
        PRIVATE (territory)                 PUBLIC (map)
        ------------------                  ------------
        preserved copies                    our synthesis
        scraped artefacts                   the connections
        full translations                   citations + metadata
        OCR'd texts, transcripts            links to good sources
        ------------------                  ------------
        purpose: survive source death       purpose: be findable
        audience: named readers             audience: anyone
        rights: reproduction only           rights: ours
```

**Storage is not distribution, and that is a real legal line.** Copyright grants four rights separately: reproduction, distribution, public display, and derivative works. A private stash engages exactly one — reproduction. Distribution is where statutory damages live (up to $150,000 per work for willful infringement, available without proof of actual loss). For a purely private copy, the rights holder must prove actual damages, which for material that is freely viewable is close to unprovable. **The realistic worst case for a private stash is a takedown demand. The realistic worst case for a public mirror is statutory damages.**

**The three tiers.**

- **Tier 0 — never publish.** Third-party book PDFs and scraped artifacts; extracted text from copyrighted works; our claim-status records and analyses concerning named people and institutions; our own legal analysis; raw harvested reference data; internal indexes; the research queue.
- **Tier 1 — review before publishing.** Any full-text reproduction of a third-party work; any health or medical claim, however framed; any negative factual claim about an identifiable living person; declassified material naming a third country; trademark uses that imply endorsement.
- **Tier 2 — publish freely.** Metadata (facts are not copyrightable); short quotations with attribution; our own registers; our analysis of *ideas*; links out to where a work legitimately lives; public-domain works; permissioned material; our own translations once authorized.

**The rules, consolidated.**

1. Never distribute the stash. Named custodian, named readers, no public links.
2. Document purpose and provenance **at the moment of preservation**, not after a complaint. A dated record saying "preserved on this date because the source is fragile" is worth more than any after-the-fact explanation.
3. Maintain a takedown process that actually works. **Being reachable and prompt is the cheapest mitigation there is.**
4. **No mirroring.** Mirroring copyrighted material multiplies the risk — every copy is another reproduction. An encrypted offline backup is defensible; three public copies are not.
5. **Publish the map, never the territory.**
6. Track permission status per item as a first-class field, not a note.
7. Check a source's terms before bulk acquisition.
8. Storage is not distribution — **and neither is it permission.** Both halves of that sentence matter.

**The trap that catches most people: free to view is not free to use.**

| Status | Meaning | Publishable? |
|---|---|---|
| **Permissioned** | We hold a grant from the rights holder | Yes, to the extent granted — check the scope |
| **Public domain** | The copyright has expired in the work itself | Yes |
| **Publicly viewable** | Free to watch on YouTube | **No.** Viewable is not licensed. Transcription is a derivative work. |
| **Unknown** | Not established | Private stash only, until established |

**What removal does and does not do.** Removal stops ongoing and future exposure, demonstrates good faith, and resets the boundary. It does **not** undo publication — anything already fetched stays fetched, in search caches, archives, and scrapes. And one caveat worth knowing: in a defamation context, quiet rapid removal can be read as consciousness of liability. That is not a reason to leave something wrong standing; it is a reason to **document the reason for a removal at the time it happens.** A documented reason is defensible. A silent deletion is a different thing.

**The honest limit, stated plainly:** we are not a qualifying library or archive, so the archival exemptions do not cover us. Preservation is a legitimate *purpose* — it bears on fair use and on willfulness — but it is not a legal permission. The sentence we can say, and can defend, is: **"We preserve what exists so it survives its source. We publish what we found."**

**Why this is worth doing at all.** When public material goes, it tends to go everywhere at once. In our own count this year, **twenty of one researcher's roughly three hundred YouTube lectures disappeared inside an eleven-day window.** *(That figure is our own measurement, not an external statistic.)* That is not hypothetical fragility.

**And the framing rule that ties all of it together:** *the document is a claim; the record is the proof.* A record is provable. A verdict has to be defended. So we publish **statuses and their basis** rather than pronouncements — and every claim carries its source and its confidence, and we record dissenting mechanisms alongside the accepted one.

## Part 3 — The cross-border layer

Applies to every member who lives outside the US and is considering a US entity. **The advice to "set up a Wyoming corporation instead of operating from where you live" usually has two expensive holes in it.**

### Hole 1 — Form 5472, and a $25,000 penalty

A US single-member LLC owned wholly by a foreign person is a *disregarded entity* — but since 2017 (TD 9796) it is also a **"reporting corporation."** So it must:

- File **Form 5472 with a pro forma Form 1120**, by April 15, **mailed or faxed** (it cannot be e-filed)
- **$25,000 penalty** for failure to file, an incomplete filing, or failure to keep records
- After 90 days' notice, **another $25,000 per 30-day period, with no statutory cap**, per related party

**It applies with no US income, no US bank account, and no US operations.** The trigger is a *reportable transaction* with the foreign owner — and **contributions and distributions between the LLC and its owner count.** Running a US LLC from abroad generates exactly those transactions.

This is on page one of the IRS instructions. It is not in the sales pitch.

### Hole 2 — you cannot incorporate your way out of where you work

**Indonesia** (and a great many other countries) applies a **place-of-management** test. A company is an Indonesian tax resident if it is established in Indonesia **OR has its place of management in Indonesia** — alternatives, not cumulative. So a Wyoming company managed from Bali can be an Indonesian resident taxpayer.

**PER-23/PJ/2025, effective 9 December 2025**, adopts substance over form and locates effective management where strategic decisions are *actually taken*. The standard illustration: board minutes signed abroad do not settle the question if the decisions recorded in them were made at home.

If a permanent establishment exists: 22% corporate tax **plus a 20% branch profits tax** on after-tax profit, whether or not repatriated. PE triggers include a place of management, an office for promotion and selling, services furnished by employees beyond 60 days in twelve months, a dependent agent, and computers or automated equipment used to conduct business over the internet.

**A US layer does not remove a local management presence. It adds a second jurisdiction's filing obligations on top of the one that was already there.**

**Also separate, and not fixable by paperwork:** immigration. Doing business in a country on the wrong visa is its own problem, and enforcement is real.

### And "one of us could take hold of it"

**Legitimate:** a **registered agent** (a state requirement, usually a service company), or an **appointed manager or director with real authority, a written agreement, and disclosure as beneficial owner**. Ordinary practice everywhere.

**Not legitimate: a nominee.** Someone holds the entity for another person, undisclosed. Banks, Stripe, and payment processors run beneficial-ownership checks, so the omission is where civil exposure becomes fraud. The nominee personally carries the liability, including the $25,000 penalties above. It can be tax evasion for both parties. And it weakens a private-association posture rather than strengthening it — an entity purporting to be membership-held while being controlled by one undisclosed person is a worse position, not a better one.

**The test:** does the holder have real authority, and is the arrangement disclosed? If the answer to either is no, stop.

## Part 5 — How to contribute

**Documents.** Send us a **link**, not a copy, wherever the work legitimately lives. Along with it, three facts: what it is (title, author, date, language), where it came from, and what you know about its permission status (permissioned / public domain / publicly viewable / unknown).

What happens next is the same every time: if the source is fragile, we preserve a copy **privately** so it survives its source dying. What we publish is the metadata, the connections, and the link — never the text, unless permission exists. Every preserved item gets its purpose and provenance recorded on the day it arrives, and its permission status tracked as a field.

- **Channel:** *(to be set — this is the one line we did not want to invent.)* A GitHub issue on the archive repository works today. An email address is better if you want one; tell us.

**Research requests.** A good request is **one question with a scope**. "What is the evidence that X" gets a real answer; "tell me about X" gets a summary. What comes back is a dated record: each claim, its source, its confidence, and what would change our mind.

**Corrections.** If we have something wrong, say so. The correction goes *into* the record — it does not get quietly deleted.
